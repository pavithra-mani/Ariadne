The crop is affected by many offoliar diseases, among which various blight diseases caused by different pathogens are the most serious diseases and cause leaf yield loss up to 15 % besides deteriorating the leaf quality. The blight symptoms are manfestedon various parts ofplants i.e., leaf stem, shoot, bud etc. A number of blight diseases such as Alternaria leaf blight, Fusarium leaf blight, Helminthosporium leafblight, Colletotrichum leafblight, Scierotium blights, bud blight, twig blight, branch blight, leaf roll blight, bacterial leaf blight and so on caused by various fungal and bacterial pathogens were reported on mulberryfrom various parts of the world. ## Introduction

Diseases are one of the major limiting factors in successful cultivation of many crops including mulberry (Morus spp.). Mulberry is a perennial plant cultivated as bush or tree for its foliage to rear silkworms in tropical and subtropical countries. The crop is vulnerable to various diseases caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses, mycoplasma like organisms, nematoda, etc. These diseases are either airborne (foliar diseases) or soilborne (root diseases) in nature reducing the leaf yield up to 20% besides deteriorating the quality. Major diseases affecting the foliage are leaf spot, powdery mildew, leaf rust and blight. Surveys conducted by various authors have revealed that blight diseases are prevalent throughout the year in all the countries where sericulture is practiced causing substantial loss in leaf yield both under rainfed and irrigated cultivation systems (Teotia and Sen, 1994; Gunasekhar et al., 1 994a; Philip etal., 1994; 1997; Minamizawa, 1997; Maji and Qadri, 1999; Ertian, 2003). The blight appears in the form of browning/ blackening/burning of various parts of plants like leaf, stem and bud. Both fungi and bacteria are reported to cause various blight diseases in mulberry. Based on the symptoms appearing on various parts of the plant, the blight diseases are named as leaf blight, stem blight, twig blight, trunk blight, shoot blight, bud blight, etc. Blight incidence is more severe in when plants are cultivated under closer spacing system and affects the nutritional value of leaf by reducing moisture, protein, chlorophyll content and total sugars. Feeding silkworms with infected/nutritionally imbalanced leaves adversely affects yield and commercial characters of cocoon (Rao et al., 1981; Sastry, 1984; Siddaramaiah and Hegde, 1990; Raju, 1998). This review elucidates the research so far conducted on various blight diseases of the mulberry and the priority areas for future research are also identified, as much work remains to be done on the management of the blight diseases.

## Fungal Leaf Blight

The fungal leaf blight caused by various species ofAlternaria, Fusarium, Helminthosporium and Colletotrichum belonging to various genera is prevalent mainly in tropical regions. The leaf blight caused by species of Alternaria and Fusarium is most prevalent in India, China and Japan (Philip et al., 1994; Minamizawa, 1997; Gupta, 2001; Ertian, 2003) whereas blight caused by species of Colletotrichum and Helminthosporium are prevalent in India (Gupta etal., 1997; Latha etal., 2000). These fungal leaf blights cause leaf yield loss up to 10% deteriorating the leaf quality. Based on the fungal pathogens the leaf blights are categorized as follows.

(I) Alternaria leaf blight:

Occurrence of mulberry leaf blight caused byAlternaria alternata (Fr.) Keissler belonging to the family Dematiaceae, order Moniliales of class Deuteromycetes was first time reported by Gunasekhar et al. (1994b) from India. Though the disease is more prevalent in peninsular India throughout the year, the severity of disease is more remarkable during summer season. Temperature of25-30° C and relative humidity of 40-60 % are favourable for the outbreak of the fungal blights (Raju, 1998). The disease is characterized by drying (blackening/browning) of the leaves starting from the leaf tip and extending to the base, covering the entire leaf lamina, and then to the petiole. As disease advances, severely affected leaves wither and fall-off. The inoculum is mainly air-borne, spreading by means of conidia, which infect the leaves causing deterioration of tissues leading to blight symptoms.

Conidiophores and conidia of the fungus are yellowish-brown in colour. Conidia are narrow, ovoid to ellipsoidal, measuring 8-12 26-27 m, with 3-8 transverse septa and 1-3 longitudinal septa.

Conidia germinate by producing more than one germ tube, which terminate into finger-like projections called appressoria. These appressoria help the pathogen to enter the leaf tissue by direct penetration through leaf cuticle and epidermis or through wounds (Gupta et al., 1998). Alternaria remains dormant in infected leaves and survives in soil on plant debris infecting the new crop when the conditions are congenial. Among the various fungicides used for the control of Alternaria blight, foliar spray of 0.2 % Dithane M-45 (mancozeb 75 % WP) has been found to be effective against the disease. Bioagents like Trichoderma harzianum, T. pseudokoningii and Gliocladium virens inhibited the mycelial growth of A. alternata up to 75 % under in vitro conditions (Raju, 1998).

## Bacterial Leaf Blight

The disease is caused by a bacterium, Pseudomonas syringae pv. mon (Boyer et Lambert) Young belonging to the order Pseudomonadales ofclass Schizomycetes. Bacterium mon (Boyer et Lambert) and Pseudomonas mon (Boyer et Lambert) Steven are synonyms of the P. syringae pv. mon (Maji and Qadri, 1999). The first occurrence of bacterial blight of mulberry was reported from Japan (Hori, 1906). Later, the disease was reported by Doidge (1915) from South Africa, Christoff(1930) from Bulgaria, Dowson (1932) from Tasmania, Sinha and Saxena (1966) from India, Amani and Akhavizadegan (1979) from Iran, Novak and Lanzova (1983) from Czechoslovakia, Zing et al. (1988) from China, Akthar and Sarwar (1988) from Pakistan, Fernandes (1999) from Portugal and Sahin et al. (1999) from Turkey. In India and Japan the disease is known as bacterial blight while in China it is familiar as black blight/shrinkage blight (Krishna Prasad and Siddaramaiah, 1978; Minamizawa, 1997; Ertian, 2003). Further, species of Xanthornonas like X campestris pv. mon (Pommel) Dowson andX. campestris pv. rnonicola is another series of bacterial pathogen causing leaf blight of mulberry in India (Kore and Patil, 1985; Nagaraj, 1997; Maji et al., 1998; Nagaraj et al., 2000; Ravikumar et al., 2004). Sharma et al. (2000) observed that P. syringae pv. Mori and X campestris pv. morE were found to be associated with bacterial blight disease causing disease complex. All types of bacterial blights are more prevalent during rainy season followed by winter season and about 8-12% leaf yield loss is observed every year besides deteriorating the leaf quality. Atmospheric temperature fluctuating between 20-30° C and high humidity (more than 80 %) are favourable for disease development while rainfall and relative humidity has direct relation with disease development (Sato and Takahashi, 1973; Takahashi, 1980; Zing et al., 1988; Sengupta et al., 1990; Teotia and Mandal, 1993; Teotia and Sen, 1994; Gunasekhar et al., 1994a; Gangwar and Thangavelu, 1991; 1995; Sharma et al., 2000). The disease drastically reduces feeding value of leaves (Sato and Takahashi, 1973).

The symptoms appear as numerous small water-soaked, irregular brown spots on the lower surface of leaves, which later spread to the upper leaf surface also. Under severe conditions, the spots become big and poly angular. Veins and veinlets become necrotic. Finally the leaves become curled, rotten and turn to brownish-black in colour. The leaves get crinkled, distorted, curved outward and fall prematurely (Krishna Prasad and Siddaramaiah, 1978; Minamizawa, 1997; Ertian, 2003). ## Minor Fungal/Bacterial Blight Diseases

The disease, which occurs occasionally and restricted to certain areas only, is called minor blight disease. Time-to-time a number of such diseases have been reported in India and abroad. In India, mulberry seedlings are found to be more vulnerable to the attack ofAlternaria a/tern ala (Fr.) Keissler causing seedling blight. The symptoms include drying of seedlings from leaf tip by burning, which is extended to the leaf base with brownish concentric rings at periphery and entering the main stem with petiole within 10-15 days (Gunasekhar etal., 1992). Fungal pathogens such as Phoma exigua (Dayakar Yadav and Sukumar, 1987), Mycosphaerella ulmi (KIeb.) (Misra et al., 1994) and Cladosporium chiorocephalum (Fresen.) Mason et Ellis (Chowdary et al., 2005) and bacterial pathogen like X campestris pv. mon (Gupta el al., 2001) are reported to cause shoot/stem blight disease in mulberry with an average intensity of 8 % in different mulberry varieties. P. exigua affects tender portions of stem causing discoloration and blackening of bark leading to sudden withering of branches and defoliation. M. ulmi is manifested as numerous reddish white lesions on the lower portion of mulberry twigs which later become hard, circular brick red to red lesions and cover the entire twig. C. chiorocephalum appears on tender stem of mulberry in the form of brownish black, round to oval spots, which irregularly spread to the entire shoot in severe condition, when the garden is affected with pest attack. As the disease progresses the affected portion becomes black and irregular in shape. Under severe conditions, the fungus causes death and decay of the affected tissues. X. campestris pv. mori appears as marginal burning of leaf lamina and spread to the stem/shoot resulting in drooping of the entire shoot. So far, no control measures have been reported for seedling blight and shoot/stem blight diseases except stem blight caused by P. exigua, which can be controlled by removal of infected branches and foliar spray of 0.2 % carbendazim (50 % WP) at the time of sprouting (Sukumar and Padma, 1999).

In Japan, Takahashi and Sato(1977) reported halo blight caused by a new strain of Pseudomonas mon. The disease appears as necrotic spot with halo on leaves and general chlorosis of young shoot. Dogare shoot blight caused by Diaporthe nomurai Hara, branch blight [Stiginina mon (Nomura) Shirata et Takahashi] and southern blight (Corticium rolfsii Curzi) are sporadic blight diseases. The symptoms appear on the shoot as circular, oval, spindle or irregular shaped markings with oily appearance whereas branch blight occurs in the shoot branch in the form of dark, blackish, brown tubercies. Burning of diseased shoots and avoiding open injuries to the shoots can prevent the disease. Southern blight appears in the form ofrottening of shoot with sclerotial bodies of the fungus (Fukuchi and Mitsueda, 1982; Minamizawa, 1997). However, there are no reports in detail on the epidemiology and control measures of these pathogens.

## Impact Of Agronomical Practices On Blight Diseases

In India, various culturallagronomical practices i. e., plant spacing, pruning methods, balanced application of fertilizers, weed management etc. are known to reduce disease severity of fungal (A. alternata / F. pallidoroseum) and bacterial blights (P. syringae pv. mori/X campestris pv. mori) to some extent in mulberry. Planting of mulberry with wider spacing (90 x 90 cm) and paired row planting system (150 + 90 x 60 cm) helps to reduce the incidence of fungal and bacterial blights to an extent of 23-30% over the closer spacing (60 x 60 cm! 60 x 30cm), which creates ideal condition for multiplication and spreading of pathogens (Sharma etal., 1993; 1996). Similarly, about 21-25 % reduction of disease severity was observed under middle pruning (40 cm above the ground level) over basal pruning. Higher disease incidence in ground level pruned plots might be due to easy entry of pathogens/over wintering stage of pathogen present in soilldebris through cut ends of the plants (Sharma et al., 1993; 1996). Significantly lesser incidence (40-45 %) of blight diseases is recorded under fertilizer application with high amount of phosphorus (300: 180:120 kg NPK), application of bacterial biofertilizers (BBF) and VAM over recommended fertilizer application (300: 120: 120 kg NPK) (Sharma eta!, 1994; 1995; 1996; Govindaiah and Sharma, 2001). The weed control is important in disease management as they act as alternative hosts for various blight pathogens of mulberry. In the absence of mulberry leaves (after pruning/harvesting the leaves), these pathogens survive on many dicot weeds and are able to spread the disease to mulberry. The crop debris is one of the main sources of inoculum in mulberry garden. Crop debris left in the garden often possesses inoculum of the pathogens in the form of spores and mycelia, which remain inactive during unfavorable condition and infect the plant when conditions become favourable. Hence debris should suitably be disposed either by burning or by composting (Sharnia- unpublished).

## Effect Of Diseased Leaves On Silkworm Health

Healthy growth and development of silkworms largely depends upon the quality of mulberry leaves fed. Many workers have reported that nutritional quality of mulberry leaves influence the growth and cocoon production to a great extent (Qadri et al., 1998; 1999). Biochemical and physiological studies have revealed that blight affected mulberry leaves are poor in nutritive value due to reduction in leaf moisture, protein, sugar contents, etc. Feeding of blight infected leaves to silkworms affects the rearing, reeling and grainage parameters (Raju, 1998). The loss of economic characters might be due to the feeding of poor quality leaves especially deficient in protein, which is one of the most essential nutrients for silkworms as the leaf protein is converted into silk protein (sericin and fibroin) through biological process in silkworm (Fukuda etal., 1959; Takeuchi, 1960).

## Spray Volume And Residual Toxicity On Silkworm

The spray volume required for blight diseases of mulberry are classified into three groups such as high volume (HV), low volume (LV) and ultra low volume (ULV). The required solution of fungicide per hectare for spraying on the foliage in respect to HV, LV and ULV ranges from 450-600 litres, 250400 litres and 5 litres, respectively. The HV technique can be used when the disease is very severe in mulberry gardens (more than 50 %), while LV can be adopted in fields moderately affected (less than 50 %) by the disease. However, for prophylactic/preventive measure, the ULV technique can be followed (Sharma - unpublished). After spraying of fungicides, the residual effect on silkworms is the main concern as silkworms are very sensitive to chemicals. The bioassay study of sprayed fungicide should be conducted before using the mulberry leaves for silkworm rearing. The recommended fungicides used for the control of blight diseases in mulberry do not have residual toxicity for longer duration and sprayed leaves can safely be used for silkworm rearing after one week of foliar spraying. If a new fungicide is being tested/tried and safe period is not known in such case, the tentative safe period could be found out by observing the different coloured triangular marks on fungicide pack such as red/yellow, blue and green where the safe period to be followed as 15-20, 5-7 and 3-5 days, respectively.

## Strategies For Tackling Blight Diseases

To achieve the desired level of control measures, knowledge on correct and accurate diagnosis of the disease is required. This criterion can save the plant from the disease by adoption of suitable control measures. All over the world, the most common and effective method for the control of blight diseases in mulberry is chemical fungicides. The approaches of biological control and botanicals are mostly effective in the laboratory and in the green house conditions but these approaches are less successful in the field especially when the intensity of disease is very high. Therefore, it is necessary to develop an integrated disease management strategy by combining all the possible components like judicious application of fungicides (sub-lethal dose), effective botanicals and biocontrol agents for the control of blight diseases in mulberry. Further, there is a need to study different isolates of blight pathogens and host pathogen interaction for developing effective disease management strategies. Molecular approaches like RFLP, RAPD and axenic/cell culture techniques can be employed to understand the host pathogen interaction at molecular level, so as to develop appropriate methods for tackling the blight pathogens. Research on development of disease forewarning/forecasting models suited for different agro climatic zones needs special emphasis for integrated disease management programme.
